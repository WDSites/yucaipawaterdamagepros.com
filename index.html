Not every job needs all four steps. A small supply-line leak caught in an hour may need only extraction and a few days of drying, while a slow ceiling leak can mean opening the wall before anyone knows how far it went. The assessment is what sorts one from the other, and it is the reason a quick call usually costs less than waiting.

For loss that started with sewage or floodwater, the cleanup stage grows and safety rules get stricter. Read about flood damage restoration if that describes your situation.

Nobody can quote a fair number for water damage over the phone, because two houses with the same leak can have very different outcomes. These five factors do most of the work in setting the final figure.

A general note on insurance

A burst pipe is a sudden and accidental event, and homeowners policies often respond to it. Slow leaks and neglect often are not covered, and a flood normally requires its own policy. Coverage depends on the wording, so check your policy or ask your insurer.

What to document before cleanup begins

Film and photograph each wet room with the date showing, write down what got soaked, and save receipts for anything you buy in a hurry. Do not discard ruined belongings until the insurer clears you to. The written scope we prepare then adds moisture readings and equipment logs to your own record.

Industry standards

Ask Any Company Which Standard Its Crews Follow

Two published documents shape professional work in this trade. ANSI/IICRC S500 covers water damage restoration, including how water is classified and how drying is documented. ANSI/IICRC S520 covers mold remediation.

You do not need to memorize either one. Ask the company on the phone whether its crews work to them, and ask what readings and photos you will receive. A clear answer is a good sign, and a vague one is worth a second call elsewhere.
